\section{Data and Methodology}

\subsection{Data Source and Country Samples}

This essay uses data from the 2021 Progress in International Reading
Literacy Study (PIRLS), which assesses the reading achievement of Grade~4
learners. PIRLS is conducted every five years by the International
Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA) and is
widely recognised as an international benchmark of reading achievement
and development. The first PIRLS cycle was conducted in 2001, meaning
that PIRLS~2021 provides twenty years of reading-trend data for learners
at a pivotal stage of schooling, when they are expected to transition
from learning to read towards reading to learn
\citep{mullis2023pirls}. This study compares Brazil and South Africa as
two upper-middle-income countries with similar levels of GDP per capita
\citep{spaull2023pirls}. The dataset is publicly available from the
IEA website. The statistical analysis was conducted in \texttt{R} using
reproducible scripts and the \texttt{EdSurvey} package.

\subsection{Variables and Measurement}

The outcome variable for this study is reading achievement, measured using
all five plausible values (\texttt{ASRREA01}--\texttt{ASRREA05}).
Plausible values are multiple draws from each learner's estimated
achievement distribution and are intended to produce unbiased
population-level estimates rather than precise individual scores.
Estimates are calculated across all five plausible values and combined
to incorporate uncertainty in the measurement of reading achievement.
The main explanatory variable is the bullying-frequency category
(\texttt{ASDGSB}), which classifies learners as experiencing bullying
``About Weekly,'' ``About Monthly,'' or ``Never or Almost Never.''
The continuous bullying scale (\texttt{ASBGSB}) is used as an alternative
specification of the main explanatory variable. Higher scores indicate
less frequent bullying.

\subsection{Empirical Strategy and Specifications}

Five models were estimated using progressively larger sets of controls.
Model~1 (\textbf{M1}) estimates the unadjusted association between
bullying frequency and reading achievement. Model~2 (\textbf{M2}) adds
controls for gender and age. Model~3 (\textbf{M3}) additionally controls
for home socioeconomic status (SES), while Model~4 (\textbf{M4}) adds
home language. Finally, Model~5 (\textbf{M5}) includes all the preceding
variables as well as school location and school disadvantage.

The control variables were introduced in theoretically motivated blocks.
Gender was included because previous research indicates that bullying
exposure differs between boys and girls
\citep{juan2025risk,anton_erxleben2016bullying,sbrogliorizzotto2021bullying}.
Age was included because older learners and learners who have repeated a
grade may differ in both their risk of victimisation and academic
achievement \citep{juan2025risk}. Home resources were added as a measure
of socioeconomic background, which is strongly associated with
educational achievement and may also be related to bullying exposure
\citep{sbrogliorizzotto2021bullying}. Home language was included because
alignment between the language spoken at home, the language of
instruction and the assessment language may influence reading performance
and may also capture relevant cultural or minority-group differences.
Finally, school location and school disadvantage were included to account
for differences in school resources, socioeconomic composition and safety
environments that may be associated with both bullying and achievement
\citep{anton_erxleben2016bullying,juan2025risk}.

 Because the complete-case requirements become more restrictive as controls are added, the analytical sample decreases in Models 3 and 5. As a robustness check, Models 1–4 were therefore re-estimated using the complete-case sample from Model 5. Holding the sample constant helps distinguish changes in the estimated bullying coefficients resulting from covariant adjustment from changes caused by differences in sample composition. This common-sample analysis does not eliminate possible selection bias arising from systematic missingness, but it makes comparisons across specifications more informative.


The models are estimated separately for South Africa and Brazil. For country $c$, plausible value $p$, and specification $m$, the estimating equation is

\begin{equation}
Y_{isc}^{(p)} = \alpha_{c}^{(m)}
+ \beta_{1c}^{(m)}\text{Monthly}_{isc}
+ \beta_{2c}^{(m)}\text{Weekly}_{isc}
+ \mathbf{X}_{isc,m}'\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{c}^{(m)}
+ \varepsilon_{isc}^{(p)},
\qquad p=1,\ldots,5,
\label{eq:achievement_model}
\end{equation}

In Equation~\ref{eq:achievement_model}, \(Y_{isc}^{(p)}\) denotes reading
plausible value \(p\) for learner \(i\) attending school \(s\) in country
\(c\). The superscript \(m\) identifies the model specification, with
\(m=1,\ldots,5\). The indicators \(\text{Monthly}_{isc}\) and
\(\text{Weekly}_{isc}\) identify learners classified as experiencing
bullying about monthly or about weekly, respectively, with learners who
were never or almost never bullied forming the omitted reference group.
The corresponding coefficients, \(\beta_{1c}^{(m)}\) and
\(\beta_{2c}^{(m)}\), therefore measure conditional mean differences in
PIRLS reading points relative to this reference group. The vector
\(\mathbf{X}_{isc,m}\) contains the controls included in specification
\(m\), while \(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_c^{(m)}\) contains their associated
coefficients. Finally, \(\varepsilon_{isc}^{(p)}\) is the error term.
The models are estimated separately for South Africa and Brazil and
across all five plausible values.Two additional specifications interact bullying frequency with gender (I1) and home socioeconomic status (I2) to assess whether the bullying–achievement association differs across these groups.

\subsection{Survey Design and Estimation}

All estimates incorporate the PIRLS total student sampling weight (TOTWGT), which accounts for unequal probabilities of selection and adjustments for nonresponse at the school, class and student levels. Sampling variances are estimated using the PIRLS jackknife repeated replication procedure and the supplied JKZONE and JKREP variables. This procedure accounts for the clustered and stratified survey design. The released data produce 250 replicate estimates for South Africa and 196 for Brazil. Reading-achievement estimates additionally incorporate uncertainty across the five plausible values. The analysis was implemented in R using the EdSurvey package.

\subsection{Methodological Limitations}

Several limitations should be considered. First, bullying exposure and
reading achievement were measured during the same assessment period. The
analysis therefore cannot determine which occurred first. Bullying may
contribute to poorer achievement, but lower-achieving learners may also
face a greater risk of victimisation. Unobserved family, peer and school
characteristics may additionally influence both bullying and achievement
\citep{anton_erxleben2016bullying}.

Second, bullying exposure is self-reported and may therefore be affected
by recall error, differences in learners' interpretation of the questions
and their willingness to report victimisation.

Third, the common-sample analysis reduces the number of observations
because all models are re-estimated using only learners with complete
information for Model~5. This allows changes in the bullying coefficients
to be compared without simultaneously changing the composition of the
sample. However, excluding learners with missing information may introduce
selection bias if missingness is related to bullying or reading
achievement. The smaller sample may also result in less precise estimates.

Fourth, South African and Brazilian Grade~4 learners were assessed one
year later than originally scheduled, during a period of widespread
COVID-19-related educational disruption.

Finally, Brazil and South Africa should not be treated as interchangeable
simply because both are classified as upper-middle-income countries. They
differ in their education systems, levels and patterns of inequality,
linguistic environments, school conditions and social understandings of
bullying. The estimated associations are therefore context-specific
averages and may conceal substantial heterogeneity across learners and
schools. Evidence from these two countries cannot be generalised to all
upper-middle-income countries.
